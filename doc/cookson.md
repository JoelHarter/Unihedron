# The Cookson Solids

**Discovered by Arthur J Cookson in 2026**

---

## Part 1: The Regime Philosophy

A **Cookson Solid** is not a single, rigid set of coordinates. It is a **Topological Regime**—a continuous, sliding family of shapes.

* **The Regime is the Shape**: Any two configurations that exist within the same morphing regime are mathematically the same polyhedron. (For example, a stretched rectangular box and a perfect cube belong to the exact same regime; one does not become a new shape just because it got taller).
* **No Canonical Form**: There is no mathematically "correct" or "canonical" version of a regime. Any configuration that satisfies the rules is a valid representation. Defining a reliable way to construct any valid instance of the shape (e.g., "Push Catalan $X$ to a sphere and convex-crease it") is a complete and valid definition of the entire regime.
* **Presentation is Pragmatic**: While you can pick any point in the regime to illustrate it, some configurations clearly communicate the shape's nature better than others. Presenting a confusing, squashed version is allowed by the rules, but frowned upon by common sense.

---

## Part 2: The Core Axioms (The Sieve)

To qualify as a Cookson Solid regime, the continuous family must satisfy all of the following rules at every state of its morphing path:

1. **The Spherical Anchor**: Every vertex must lie exactly on the surface of a single sphere.
2. **The Two-Polygon Limit**: The faces of the solid can consist of at most two distinct geometric types. *(Note: Left-handed and right-handed mirror images of an asymmetric face legally count as the same polygon type).*
3. **Strict Topological Graph**: The connectivity of the shape (which vertices connect to form which edges and faces) must remain entirely constant.
4. **Forced Simplification**: The geometry must always be described in its mathematically simplest form. If two adjacent faces become coplanar, they instantly merge into one face. If two vertices occupy the exact same coordinate, they instantly merge into one vertex.
5. **No Self-Intersection**: Faces and edges may not cross through one another.
6. **The Taxonomic Exclusion (The Claim Rule)**: If any valid state within a regime's continuous sliding family forms a classical solid (Platonic, Archimedean, Catalan, Johnson) or the base of an infinite geometric series (Prisms, Antiprisms, Pyramids, Bipyramids, Trapezohedra, etc.), that established set claims the entire regime. Cookson solids strictly occupy the unclaimed topological territory.

---

## Part 3: Emergent Consequences

If you rigorously apply the axioms above, the following boundaries naturally police the regime without needing to be stated as rules:

* **Concavity is Allowed**: Valley folds are completely legal as long as they don't cause the mesh to self-intersect.
* **Edges Cannot Invert**: An edge can never flip from convex to concave (or vice versa). To do so, the dihedral angle would have to pass through 180° (flat). The exact moment it goes flat, Axiom 4 triggers, the faces merge, the graph breaks, and it exits the regime.
* **Vertices Cannot Collide or Cross**: Moving one vertex onto another triggers Axiom 4, deleting a vertex, altering the graph, and exiting the regime.

## Part 4: The Known Cookson Regimes ($C_1 - C_{12}$)

Below are the regimes currently known to exist. They are defined here by a reliably reproducible construction method, but these descriptions represent the entire flexible family.

### The Convex Regimes (Art's Discoveries)
Generated by taking a parent Catalan solid, normalizing all vertices to a unit sphere, and taking the strict convex hull (choosing the "mountain fold" diagonal for all skew faces).

* **$C_1$: Trigonal Octasphere**
  * *Derivation*: Convex projection of the Rhombic Dodecahedron ($V=14, F=24$ triangles).
* **$C_2$: Trigonal Icosasphere**
  * *Derivation*: Convex projection of the Rhombic Triacontahedron ($V=32, F=60$ triangles).
* **$C_3$: Bitrigonal Octasphere**
  * *Derivation*: Convex projection of the Deltoidal Icositetrahedron ($V=26, F=48$ triangles).
* **$C_4$: Bitrigonal Icosasphere**
  * *Derivation*: Convex projection of the Deltoidal Hexecontahedron ($V=62, F=120$ triangles).
* **$C_5$: Gyrotrapezotrigonal Octasphere**
  * *Derivation*: Convex projection of the Pentagonal Icositetrahedron ($V=38, F=48$: 24 trapezoids, 24 triangles).
* **$C_6$: Gyrotrapezotrigonal Icosasphere**
  * *Derivation*: Convex projection of the Pentagonal Hexecontahedron ($V=92, F=120$: 60 trapezoids, 60 triangles).

### The Concave Regimes (The Hidden Shadows)
Generated by taking the exact same normalized Catalan vertices as above, but explicitly selecting the internal "valley fold" diagonals that cut underneath the convex hull, creating a dimpled, non-intersecting spherical geometry.

* **$C_7$: Concave Trigonal Octasphere**
  * *Derivation*: The valley-creased (concave) shadow of $C_1$ ($V=14, F=24$ triangles).
* **$C_8$: Concave Trigonal Icosasphere**
  * *Derivation*: The valley-creased (concave) shadow of $C_2$ ($V=32, F=60$ triangles).
* **$C_9$: Concave Bitrigonal Octasphere**
  * *Derivation*: The valley-creased (concave) shadow of $C_3$ ($V=26, F=48$ triangles in chiral pairs).
* **$C_{10}$: Concave Bitrigonal Icosasphere**
  * *Derivation*: The valley-creased (concave) shadow of $C_4$ ($V=62, F=120$ triangles in chiral pairs).
* **$C_{11}$: Concave Gyrotrapezotrigonal Octasphere**
  * *Derivation*: The valley-creased shadow of $C_5$. Created by cutting each skew pentakite from the tip to the lower pair of vertices, fracturing it into one central isosceles triangle and two mirrored flanking scalene triangles ($V=38, F=72$ triangles; 2 polygon types).
* **$C_{12}$: Concave Gyrotrapezotrigonal Icosasphere**
  * *Derivation*: The valley-creased shadow of $C_6$. Same 3-triangle pentakite cut as $C_{11}$, applied to the icosahedral parent ($V=92, F=180$ triangles; 2 polygon types).

## Julia API Usage

### 1. Accessing Solids
```julia
using Unihedron

# Access by index (1 to 12) or shorthand symbol (:C1 to :C12)
c1  = cookson(:C1)   # C1: Trigonal Octasphere (Convex)
c7  = cookson(:C7)   # C7: Concave Trigonal Octasphere (Valley shadow of C1)
c11 = cookson(:C11)  # C11: Concave Gyrotrapezotrigonal Octasphere (Valley shadow of C5)
c12 = cookson(:C12)  # C12: Concave Gyrotrapezotrigonal Icosasphere (Valley shadow of C6)

# By official constructor names
P1  = trigonal_octasphere()
P5  = gyrotrapezotrigonal_octasphere()
P7  = concave_trigonal_octasphere()
P11 = concave_gyrotrapezotrigonal_octasphere()
P12 = concave_gyrotrapezotrigonal_icosasphere()

# Query metadata
meta11 = cookson_info(:C11)
println(meta11.name)           # "Concave Gyrotrapezotrigonal Octasphere"
println(meta11.regime_type)    # "Concave (Valley Shadow)"
println(meta11.parent_catalan) # "Pentagonal Icositetrahedron"
```

```julia
using GLMakie

```

### 3. Two-Polygon Limit & Congruence Verification
```julia
# Verify that every Cookson solid satisfies the Two-Polygon Limit (<= 2 distinct polygon types):
for i in 1:12
    solid = cookson(i)
    # With chiral enantiomorphs counting as the same type (allow_flipped=true):
    classes = classify_faces(solid; allow_flipped=true)
    num_types = length(unique(classes))
    println("C$i ($(cookson_name(i))): $num_types polygon types (Faces: $(length(solid)))")
    @assert num_types <= 2
end
```
